The phases that are popularised but not really believable

1. Desperately wanting a relationship
2. Finding someone - relief:  all my problems are over
3. Something triggers an avoidant state
4. They start thinking about leaving
5. The avoidant decides to end the relationship
6. They are relieved to have left
7. They start to feel loneliness
8. Feeling sorry for themselves and wondering if they ever find a [partner

The 8-phase cycle you outlined is a widely recognized pattern in popular psychology and attachment-focused coaching. While the exact 8-step breakdown is a conceptual model rather than a formal clinical diagnosis, the underlying behavioral mechanics are strongly supported by Adult Attachment Theory (originally developed by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, and applied to adult romantic relationships by Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver).
The Scientific Basis (Attachment Theory)
In psychological research, what you are describing is primarily associated with dismissing-avoidant and fearful-avoidant (disorganized) attachment styles. Scientific studies on adult attachment validate several core mechanisms within your cycle:
The Approach-Avoidance Conflict (Phases 1–3): Research by psychologists like Mario Mikulincer and Phillip Shaver demonstrates that avoidantly attached individuals still possess the innate human biological drive for connection and attachment (Phase 1). However, they also hold deeply ingrained internal working models that associate intimacy with a loss of autonomy, engulfment, or inevitable rejection. When a relationship deepens (Phase 2), the intimacy itself acts as a trigger for their attachment system, activating defensive "deactivating strategies" (Phase 3).
Deactivating Strategies (Phases 4–5): Empirical studies show that when triggered, avoidant individuals unconsciously suppress attachment needs. They do this by hyper-focusing on a partner’s flaws, idealizing past partners or the "phantom ex," withdrawing emotionally, and ultimately seeking distance or ending the relationship to regulate their nervous system.
Suppression and Rebound (Phases 6–8): Research into emotional regulation shows that avoidant individuals are exceptionally good at suppressing distress immediately following a breakup (Phase 6). However, studies indicate that suppressed attachment needs do not disappear; once the perceived threat of intimacy is gone, the deactivation fades, allowing baseline loneliness and the desire for connection to resurface (Phases 7–8).
Where Popular Models Differ from Clinical Research
The 8-Step Sequence: The specific, linear 8-phase framework you listed is an informal, observational model popularized by relationship educators and social media therapists to make attachment dynamics easy to visualize—it is not a standardized, peer-reviewed clinical scale.
Subtypes Matter: In clinical literature, dismissing-avoidant individuals are less likely to experience intense, desperate longing (Phase 1) or self-pity (Phase 8), as they tend to maintain high self-reliance and genuinely convince themselves they do not need others. The intense swing between desperately wanting love, panicking when getting it, and feeling deep loneliness afterward is much more characteristic of fearful-avoidant (or anxious-avoidant) attachment.
The "All My Problems Are Over" Mindset (Phase 2): In attachment research, viewing a new partner as a total savior or solution to one's problems is typically classified as a trait of anxious-preoccupied attachment rather than pure avoidance, though it frequently appears in fearful-avoidant individuals during the initial infatuation (limerence) stage before their fear of intimacy is triggered.
