# Hans Model Of Consent (Rewritten In + / - Notation)

## Why This Model Matters

This model is not only about what behavior is happening. It clarifies whether consent is internally and relationally aligned.

It helps answer:

1 - Who does what to whom (who is giver and who is receiver)?
2 - Who benefits physically?
3 - Who has the power (why)?
4 - Who benefits emotionally, mentally, or relationally?

Motivation should be aligned with benefit. (Or is there a pressure mismatch?)

## The Core Axes

1. **WHO Action Vector (A/R):** Who performs the action, and who receives or is affected by it.
	- Internally this is actor x receiver, with four possible vectors: `me->me`, `me->you`, `you->me`, `you->you`.
2. **Benefit Split (Body) axis (B1):** Who is meant to enjoy physically/biologically: me / you.
3. **Driver axis (D):** Why it happens: because I want / because you want.
4. **Benefit Split (Meaning & Nourishment) axis (B2):** Who is meant to enjoy it emotionally, mentally, or relationally (who receives the emotional nourishment: me / you / both).

Teaching/UI note: present B1 and B2 together as one grouped check, **Benefit Split (Body vs Meaning & Nourishment)**, while keeping them as separate model dimensions.

## The Driver Axis As Vulnerability

The Driver axis (D) is not only a structural variable. It is the site of consent's most personal demand.

When the Driver is "me," that means: I am naming that I want this. Not that I allow it. Not that I tolerate it. That I *want* it.

That is an act of vulnerability.

- A Dom who says "I want to restrain you" is exposed: to judgment, rejection, or the fear of being seen as presumptuous or aggressive.
- A sub who says "I want to be restrained" is equally exposed: to shame, judgment, or the fear of being seen as weak, deviant, or complicit.

These are symmetric acts of self-disclosure. The vulnerability is not a property of one role. It belongs to whoever claims the Driver position honestly.

What this means for consent quality:
- Desire hidden behind role performance lowers consent quality. When someone acts because it is expected rather than because they want it, that is the `-` condition regardless of role.
- Naming desire is the prior act that consent depends on. Permission-giving is only meaningful when what is wanted has been named first.
- Structures that normalize desire-naming for all roles — not only the receiver — produce more accurate consent and less covert obligation.

### The Trap of "Blurry Mutuality"

A common obstacle to clean desire-naming is **"blurry mutuality"** — collapsing Driver (initiative) and Meaning (reward) together under the umbrella statement: *"We both want this."*

While mutuality sounds harmonious, unexamined blurry mutuality frequently functions as consent camouflage:
- **Diffusion of responsibility:** If "we both want it," neither person has to take the vulnerability of explicitly saying *"I want this from you."*
- **Camouflage for covert obligation:** In practice, "we both want it" often conceals an asymmetric pressure: *"You want this act, and I want you to be happy (or want to avoid conflict), so I convince myself I want it too."* This slides into approval-seeking (`2-`) or obligation-based allowing (`4-`).
- **Clean Mutuality vs. Blurry Mutuality:** Clean mutuality keeps the **Driver axis** binary (`me` or `you`) so that the person whose want catalyzed this specific action claims ownership of the ask. At the same time, the model welcomes **Meaning (B2)** as `both` — celebrating that both partners can derive profound, distinct emotional and relational fulfillment from an act driven by one person's initiative.



## Session Groundrules + Session Dynamic

- Negotiation is always equal at the base layer.
- Within that equal-negotiation base, temporary session power exchange can be assigned by the driver axis (but underneath there is always equality).
- Authority never removes stop rights, renegotiation rights, or underlying personal equality, even more: authority and initiative give the responsibility to monitor what is visble. The one receiving and giving are both always responsible for letting their partner know what is going on in the department of physical benifits in the here-and-now, and shifts in their idiea of furture enjoyment in the 5th core axis.

### Container Layer vs Ground Rules

- **Container layer:** the meta-agreement that holds the full scene frame.
- It sets role structure, time/intensity boundaries, monitoring responsibilities, and how pause/stop/exit works.

- **Ground rules:** concrete constraints inside that frame.
- They set explicit do/don't lines such as area limits, barrier/safer-sex requirements, no recording, no marks in visible zones, check-in rhythm, and safeword protocol.

Quick distinction:

- Container = the frame.
- Ground rules = the specific constraints inside the frame.

## Clean vs Mismatched Consent

- **Clean/aligned consent (+):** the named structure is live, willing, and coherent enough to continue.
- **Mismatched consent (-):** pressure, obligation, fear, or covert need has entered the named structure.

## The 48 Structural Positions

This model is not an 8-state model and not a 24-item model.

It is a $4 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$ model (equivalent to $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$):

1. WHO action vector (A/R): 4 values (`me->me`, `me->you`, `you->me`, `you->you`)
2. Benefit Split (Body): me / you
3. Driver: me / you
4. Benefit Split (Meaning): me / you / both

That gives $4 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 48$ structural positions.

The important shift is that emotional or relational meaning should not be forced into a binary. In many scenes, Benefit Split (Body) is clearer in one direction while Benefit Split (Meaning) is genuinely shared.

## Matrix Logic

The WHO action vector dimension has four values:

1. Me -> me
2. Me -> you
3. You -> me
4. You -> you

Each action vector can then combine with 12 motive-benefit suffixes:

1. body:me | drive:me | meaning:me
2. body:me | drive:me | meaning:you
3. body:me | drive:me | meaning:both
4. body:me | drive:you | meaning:me
5. body:me | drive:you | meaning:you
6. body:me | drive:you | meaning:both
7. body:you | drive:me | meaning:me
8. body:you | drive:me | meaning:you
9. body:you | drive:me | meaning:both
10. body:you | drive:you | meaning:me
11. body:you | drive:you | meaning:you
12. body:you | drive:you | meaning:both

So the full matrix is:

- 4 action vectors
- x 12 motive-benefit suffixes
- = 48 structural names

## Naming Grammar

Use one canonical naming format:

`[actor]->[receiver] | body:[me/you] | drive:[me/you] | meaning:[me/you/both]`

Examples:

- `me->you | body:me | drive:me | meaning:both`
- `you->me | body:you | drive:you | meaning:me`
- `me->me | body:me | drive:you | meaning:both`

This does three useful things:

1. It keeps the structure generative instead of forcing it into a fixed short list.
2. It makes the last option explicit in the name: `meaning:me`, `meaning:you`, or `meaning:both`.
3. It lets alignment be diagnosed separately instead of hiding it inside the name.

## Meaning Rules

The structure names the configuration. It does not yet judge whether the consent is clean.

Alignment is a second layer:

- `+` means driver, benefit, and live willingness are coherent enough to stay in play.
- `-` means pressure, obligation, fear, role-performance, or covert need has entered.

## The 8 Named States: Teaching Shortcuts

These eight states compress the four action vectors with a `+`/`-` alignment marker.
They are teaching shortcuts, not the full map.
The full 48-position model adds Benefit Split (Body), driver, and Benefit Split (Meaning) on top.

| Code | Name | Who / Driver | Risk | Mitigation |
|------|------|--------------|------|------------|
| 1+ | Direct Self-Directed Taking | I act. Because I want to. | Can become impact-blind without check-ins. | Schedule active empathy checks every 5–10 minutes. |
| 1- | Pressure-Driven Taking | I act. Because you expect it of me. | Performance pressure, resentment, emotional crash later. | Pause and restate desire in first-person ownership language. |
| 2+ | Clear Other-Directed Service | I act. Because you want it. | Giver fatigue without pacing limits. | Pre-agree stop points and intensity ceilings. |
| 2- | Approval-Seeking Service | I act. To gain approval, control, or safety. | Covert contracts, invisible debt, emotional crash. | Name hidden need directly, renegotiate before continuing. |
| 3+ | Clear Self-Receiving | You act. Because I want it. | Weak feedback can lead to drift. | Use explicit body feedback intervals. |
| 3- | Compliance-Based Receiving | You act. Because you feel you must accept. | Freeze responses, muted no, delayed harm signals. | Immediate downshift, then verbal reset to simple yes/no options. |
| 4+ | Willing Other-Expression Allowing | You act. Because you want to. I willingly allow. | Over-tolerance if stop rights are not actively used. | Check willingness repeatedly, not only at start. |
| 4- | Obligation-Based Allowing | You act. I allow because I feel I should. | Consent erosion, shutdown, trust damage. | Stop at first should-based language and reopen negotiation. |

**Critical risk condition:** if neither person has meaningful physical nor emotional enjoyment, stop. Shift to care, check safety, then repair with explicit language.

### Additional Risks And Strengths Per State

**1+** - *Strengths:* Clear ownership and direct desire reduce ambiguity when check-ins stay active. *Additional risks:* Arousal can outrun reflection, so impact on partner/context gets missed. "I asked for this" can silence later changes in willingness.

**1-** - *Strengths:* Acts as an early pressure signal that can trigger timely reset and repair. *Additional risks:* Self-pressure can mask fear of disappointing a partner. Shutdown may show up late because ownership language hides coercion.

**2+** - *Strengths:* Chosen service can build trust, attunement, and relational generosity. *Additional risks:* Service energy can drift into over-functioning before either person notices. Yes may stay verbal while body capacity quietly drops.

**2-** - *Strengths:* Reveals hidden approval or safety needs that can be renegotiated openly. *Additional risks:* Approval-seeking can create invisible contracts and future resentment. Care tasks get performed while consent quality is actually declining.

**3+** - *Strengths:* Active receiving with feedback improves precision and mutual attunement. *Additional risks:* Receiver passivity can reduce feedback frequency too much. Subtle discomfort may be missed when the giver appears competent.

**3-** - *Strengths:* Can surface freeze/compliance patterns early, enabling faster care responses. *Additional risks:* Freeze/compliance signals may look like cooperation from outside. Delayed processing can postpone repair until after emotional crash.

**4+** - *Strengths:* Willing allowing can support partner expression while preserving agency. *Additional risks:* Allowing can slowly become tolerance if check-ins are sparse. Power aesthetics can be mistaken for active consent confirmation.

**4-** - *Strengths:* Flags boundary fatigue and should-language before deeper consent erosion. *Additional risks:* Should-based allowing can erode trust even without explicit conflict. Boundary fatigue may appear as numbness rather than a clear no.

### Code Legend

Read the code in two parts: the number names who acts on whom, the sign tells you whether willingness and alignment are currently coherent.

- `1` = me→me (self-directed action)
- `2` = me→you (other-directed action)
- `3` = you→me (self-receiving position)
- `4` = you→you (other-expression allowing)
- `+` = aligned / clean willingness
- `-` = pressure / mismatch present

The most common clean anchors are still:

- `body:me | drive:me`
- `body:you | drive:you`

But the model is now wide enough to describe more complex realities:

- Benefit Split (Body) can sit with one person while Benefit Split (Meaning) is shared.
- a person can allow or offer something willingly even when the main Benefit Split (Body) is not theirs.
- self-action cases (`me->me`, `you->you`) can still matter in instruction, ritual, exhibition, witnessing, or guided play.

## The One-Breath Spoken Sentence Test

To eliminate all ambiguity, every configuration should be speakable as a single plain-English sentence:

> **"[Actor] does this to [Receiver], for [whose] body, because [who] wants it, to feed [whose] heart/mind."**

Examples:
- *"I do this to you, for my body's pleasure, because I want it, and it feeds both of our hearts."*
- *"You do this to me, for your body, because you want it, and it feeds my desire to serve you."*
- *"I restrain you, your body feels the sensation, because you asked for it, and it feeds your peace and my care."*

**The Operational Rule:** If two partners cannot say this sentence out loud together before or during a scene, the dynamic is still ambiguous and not ready for intensity.

## The 4 Confusion Traps (How Ambiguity Creeps In)

Most consent confusion in relationships and BDSM dynamics comes from collapsing two separate layers into one:

1. **The Action ↔ Driver Trap (Conflating Movement with Desire):**
   - *The Trap:* Assuming whoever is moving their hands or holding the implement must be the one who wants it.
   - *The Reality:* In service topping and bottom-driven scenes, the Actor is executing the Receiver's request. Always ask: *"Who asked for this move right now?"*

2. **The Body ↔ Meaning Trap (Conflating Sensation with Fulfillment):**
   - *The Trap:* Assuming that because someone's body is physically aroused or reacting to sensation, their heart and mind are happy.
   - *The Reality:* The body can respond physiologically while the person feels emotionally alienated, pressured, or numb. Never assume physical reaction equals emotional consent.

3. **The Blurry Mutuality Trap (Conflating Initiative with Mutual Enjoyment):**
   - *The Trap:* Hiding behind a vague *"We both want this!"* so neither person has to take the vulnerability of asking.
   - *The Reality:* Blurry mutuality camouflages covert pressure. One person must own the spark (`Driver: me/you`), while celebrating that both partners can find deep, distinct fulfillment (`Meaning: both`).

4. **The Tolerating ↔ Driving Trap (Conflating Allowance with Wanting):**
   - *The Trap:* Thinking *"I didn't say no or use my safeword, so I must have driven this."*
   - *The Reality:* Tolerating or allowing is passive permission (`Vector 4`); Driving is active desire (`Driver`). Stop rights must not replace genuine wanting.


## Implications For Training

For training, do not start by teaching 48 names.

Teach in layers:

1. First teach the four action vectors.
2. Then add Benefit Split (Body) and driver, because that is where most consent confusion lives.
3. Then add Benefit Split (Meaning) as the advanced layer, especially the `both` option.
4. Only after that introduce `+` and `-` as live diagnostic markers.

This keeps the model teachable without flattening it.

## Implications For Treatment And Repair

For coaching, therapy, or repair work, the expanded model is more useful than the 8-state shorthand.

It helps locate exactly where the mismatch happened:

1. Was the action vector unclear?
2. Was Benefit Split (Body) misnamed?
3. Did the driver shift under pressure?
4. Did one person assume shared meaning when the other person did not?
5. Did the structure stay the same while the `+`/`-` condition changed?

That makes it suitable for:

- debriefs,
- consent repair,
- D/s training,
- relational pattern tracking,
- trauma-aware or freeze-aware review.

## Play-State Diagnostic (Pressure vs Play)

Use this in real time. If two or more answers are "no," pause and renegotiate.

1. Is this still actively wanted, not just tolerated?
2. Can both people pause or stop without penalty?
3. Is the driver (why) explicit and still true right now?
4. Is at least one meaningful benefit channel present for each person (physical or emotional)?
5. Is anyone performing for approval, fear, or role pressure instead of willingness?
6. Do both people still know what state they are in (+ or -)?

If uncertain, use this reset line:

- "Pause. I want to check whether this is still play-state or if pressure has entered."

## Appearance vs Structure

In consensual play-state, appearances can be intentionally misleading.

- What it can look like: control, surrender, force, helplessness, or intensity.
- What matters structurally: explicit negotiation, live willingness, active stop rights, and clear motive-benefit alignment.

This means behavior alone is not enough to evaluate consent quality. The same visible action can be either:

- ethical play-state (structured, negotiated, willing), or
- pressure-state (mismatch, fear, obligation, or consent drift).

Always evaluate structure before appearance.

## Pleasure Channels Must Be Explicit

For both people, track both channels:

- physical/biological enjoyment
- emotional/mental enjoyment

These can differ in intensity. Mental enjoyment can be high while physical enjoyment is neutral, and vice versa.

## Critical Red Condition

If neither person has meaningful physical nor emotional enjoyment, stop.

Then:

1. downshift or end,
2. move to care,
3. run repair conversation,
4. renegotiate only with explicit yes.

## Practical Consent Wording

### Before starting

- "We are equal negotiators."
- "For this session, you hold initiative for X minutes."
- "Either of us can pause or stop at any moment."

### Request clarity

- "I want to do X to you for Y minutes."
- "This is for my physical enjoyment and shared emotional connection."
- "Would you like that?"

### Serving clarity

- "I can offer X for your benefit at this intensity and duration."
- "If my willingness changes, I will pause and tell you directly."

### Stop and repair

- "Stop. My willingness dropped."
- "I notice mismatch between who benefits and why this is happening."
- "I need a reset before deciding whether to continue."

## Debrief Prompts

- What did your body enjoy, tolerate, or resist?
- What felt emotionally meaningful, neutral, or pressuring?
- Did driver and beneficiary stay aligned?
- Did session authority stay within negotiated boundaries?
- What one wording change will increase clarity next time?

## Summary

This model upgrades consent from behavior labeling to alignment diagnostics.

It does not only ask what happened.
It names the full structure first, then asks whether motive, benefit, authority, and enjoyment stayed coherent for both people.

## Mapping Betty Martin's Wheel of Consent to the Cube Model

The Cube of Consent fundamentally builds upon Betty Martin's Wheel of Consent but expands it to capture the nuances of *why* an action is happening, *who* is getting physical vs. emotional benefits, and whether the interaction is genuinely aligned or under pressure.

While Betty Martin uses 2 axes (Who is doing / Who is it for) to create **4 positions**, the Cube Model expands this into **4 dimensions** (with WHO action vector internally built from actor x receiver), resulting in a $4 \\times 2 \\times 2 \\times 3$ matrix (equivalent to $2 \\times 2 \\times 2 \\times 2 \\times 3$) with **48 distinct structural positions**.

### The 8 Named States (Teaching Shortcuts)
The Cube Model translates the 4 Betty Martin positions into 4 "Action Vectors" and immediately adds a critical alignment marker: **`+` (clean/aligned consent)** or **`-` (mismatched/pressured consent)**. 

This creates 8 core teaching states:

**1. Taking (Actor: Me, Driver: Me)**
*   **1+ Direct Self-Directed Taking:** I act, because I want to. (Aligned, clean willingness)
*   **1- Pressure-Driven Taking:** I act, but because you expect it of me. (Performance pressure, fear of disappointing, resentment)

**2. Giving / Service (Actor: Me, Driver: You)**
*   **2+ Clear Other-Directed Service:** I act, because you want it. (Aligned service, builds trust)
*   **2- Approval-Seeking Service:** I act to gain approval, control, or safety. (Covert contracts, invisible debt, emotional crash)

**3. Receiving (Actor: You, Driver: Me)**
*   **3+ Clear Self-Receiving:** You act, because I want it. (Aligned receiving with active feedback)
*   **3- Compliance-Based Receiving:** You act, because I feel I must accept. (Freeze response, muted 'no', delayed harm)

**4. Allowing (Actor: You, Driver: You)**
*   **4+ Willing Other-Expression Allowing:** You act, because you want to. I willingly allow. (Aligned allowing, active consent)
*   **4- Obligation-Based Allowing:** You act. I allow because I feel I should. (Boundary fatigue, consent erosion, shutdown)

### The Full 48-Position Cube Model
The limitation of the 4-position Wheel is that "Who it's for" (the Beneficiary) is often too simplistic. The Cube Model solves this by splitting the "Beneficiary" into three separate dimensions. 

To find any of the 48 positions, you answer these 4 dimensional questions:

1. **WHO Action Vector (A/R):** Who performs the action, and who receives or is affected by it? *(Me->Me / Me->You / You->Me / You->You)*
2. **Benefit Split (Body) Axis (B1):** Who is meant to enjoy it physically/biologically? *(Me / You)*
3. **Driver Axis (D):** Why is it happening: because I want it, or because you want it? *(Me / You)*
4. **Benefit Split (Meaning) Axis (B2):** Who is meant to enjoy it emotionally, mentally, or relationally? *(Me / You / Both)*

### Why the Cube's Expansion Matters
By separating these dimensions, the Cube Model can describe complex realities that the Wheel of Consent struggles to label. For example:
*   **Shared Meaning:** In a D/s scene, Benefit Split (Body) (B1) might strictly be for one person, but Benefit Split (Meaning) (B2) is genuinely shared by `Both`. 
*   **Diagnosing "Bad" Consent:** If an interaction goes wrong, the Cube Model helps pinpoint exactly *where* the mismatch happened. Was Benefit Split (Body) misnamed? Did the driver shift under pressure? Did someone assume shared meaning when the other person didn't?
